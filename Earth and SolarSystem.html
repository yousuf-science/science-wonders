Theory — Orbits, Satellites & Solar Systems

Why Satellites Don't Fall Down (Actually, They Do)

The International Space Station is in free fall — there is nothing holding it up. Astronauts inside float not because there's no gravity (Earth's gravity is still 89% as strong at the ISS as on the ground), but because the station, the astronauts, and everything inside it are all falling together. The trick is that the ISS is moving sideways at 7.66 km/s — fast enough that as it falls toward Earth, the curved surface of Earth falls away beneath it at exactly the same rate. It never hits the ground.

Newton imagined this with a cannonball fired horizontally from a mountain. Fired slowly, it lands nearby. Fired faster, it lands further. Fired fast enough — the cannonball misses Earth entirely and comes round behind you. That is an orbit. Every satellite is just a very fast cannonball.

Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation (1687)

Every object with mass pulls on every other object with mass. The force is proportional to the product of the masses and falls off as the square of the distance.

F = G · M · m / r²
G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²·kg⁻² — Newton's gravitational constant
M, m = masses in kilograms · r = distance between centres in metres

Circular Orbital Velocity

A body in a circular orbit moves fast enough that gravity exactly supplies the centripetal acceleration needed to curve its straight-line motion into a circle. Setting gravity equal to centripetal force and solving for speed gives:

v = √( G · M / r )
Closer orbit → faster speed. The ISS at 400 km altitude orbits at 7.66 km/s; geostationary satellites at 35,786 km altitude orbit at just 3.07 km/s.

Orbital Period — Kepler's Third Law (1619)

The time for one complete orbit comes from dividing the circle's circumference by the orbital speed:

T = 2π · √( r³ / G·M )
Kepler's Third Law: T² ∝ r³. Doubling the orbital radius makes the period 2√2 ≈ 2.83 times longer.
ISS at r = 6,771 km → T = 92.7 minutes. GEO at r = 42,164 km → T = 24 hours exactly (that's why GEO satellites stay over one spot).

Eccentricity & the Shape of Orbits — Kepler's First Law

Most orbits are not perfect circles. They are ellipses, with the central body at one of the two foci. The eccentricity e tells you how stretched the ellipse is:

e = 0 — perfect circle (rare in reality)
0 < e < 1 — bound elliptical orbit (most satellites and planets)
e = 1 — parabolic trajectory (exactly escape velocity)
e > 1 — hyperbolic flyby (faster than escape — never returns)

For an elliptical orbit, the closest point to the central body is the periapsis (called perigee for Earth orbits, perihelion for solar orbits) and the furthest is the apoapsis. The live readout above computes these for every body you launch.

Escape Velocity

If you fire something fast enough, gravity can no longer bring it back. The threshold is escape velocity:

v_esc = √( 2·G·M / r ) = √2 · v_circular
From Earth's surface: 11.2 km/s. From the ISS altitude: 10.84 km/s. From the Sun at 1 AU: 42.1 km/s.

The condition for escape can be stated cleanly in terms of total energy: if the body's kinetic energy exceeds its gravitational potential energy (ε = v²/2 − GM/r > 0), it will escape and never return.

Why the ISS and Geostationary Satellites Behave So Differently

Both are in stable orbits, but at radically different altitudes — and this changes everything. The ISS at 400 km circles Earth 16 times a day. A geostationary satellite at 35,786 km circles exactly once per day, and because Earth rotates at the same rate, it appears fixed in the sky. This is why every TV satellite dish on the planet points to the same patch of sky: they're all looking at the same ring of geostationary satellites above the equator.

To go from LEO to GEO requires a manoeuvre called a Hohmann transfer: fire engines once to enter an elliptical orbit that touches both altitudes, then fire again at the high point to circularise. This is the cheapest fuel route between any two orbits.

Natural vs Artificial Satellites

The Moon is a natural satellite — captured (or co-formed with Earth) 4.5 billion years ago. It orbits at 384,400 km with a period of 27.3 days. Compare this with the artificial satellites: there are over 10,000 active satellites in Earth orbit as of 2026, ranging from LEO (300–2,000 km), through MEO (where GPS lives, around 20,200 km), to GEO (35,786 km), and a handful in highly elliptical orbits like Russia's Molniya satellites.

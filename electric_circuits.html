Theory — How Circuits Really Work

Ohm's Law — The Foundation

Every component in a circuit follows one simple rule: the current through it is proportional to the voltage across it, divided by its resistance.

V = I × R
V is voltage in volts, I is current in amperes, R is resistance in ohms. Memorise this — it unlocks every other formula.

Series — Same Current, Voltage Divides

Components in series share the same current. The voltage drop across each one adds up to the total battery voltage. Total resistance is the simple sum:

Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …
Add another bulb in series → total R goes up → current goes down → all bulbs get dimmer. This is why old Christmas lights all die when one bulb fails.

Parallel — Same Voltage, Current Divides

Components in parallel share the same voltage. The current splits between them, with more current flowing through the smaller resistance. Total resistance follows the reciprocal rule:

1/Rtotal = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + …
Adding more resistors in parallel lowers total resistance — counterintuitive but true. Houses are wired in parallel, so each appliance gets the full mains voltage independently.

Capacitors — Storage, Not Resistance

A capacitor stores charge. When you connect it across a battery, current flows briefly while it charges, then stops once the capacitor's voltage matches the battery's. Time scale follows the RC time constant:

τ = R × C
τ (tau) is the time constant in seconds. After 1τ, the capacitor reaches ~63% of full charge. After 5τ, essentially fully charged.

Capacitors in parallel add (more area, more charge): Ctotal = C₁ + C₂ + …

Capacitors in series divide (reciprocal, like parallel resistors): 1/Ctotal = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + …

Wires Aren't Perfect — R = ρL/A

Real wires have resistance, and it depends on three things: the material's resistivity ρ (rho), the wire's length L, and its cross-sectional area A.

R = ρ × L / A
Long thin wires resist more. Short thick wires resist less. This is why power transmission uses thick copper cables: less heating, less wasted energy. Turn on "Realistic Wires" in the toolbar to see this in action.
Copperρ ≈ 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m
Aluminiumρ ≈ 2.65 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m
Ironρ ≈ 9.71 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m
Nichromeρ ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m (heater wire)

Ammeters, Voltmeters, Galvanometers

Ammeter measures current. It must be placed in series with the wire whose current you want to measure. An ideal ammeter has zero resistance, so it doesn't affect the circuit.
Voltmeter measures voltage between two points. It must be placed in parallel across them. An ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance, so no current flows through it.
Galvanometer is a sensitive current detector. It deflects left or right depending on the direction of current. A famous use: the Wheatstone bridge, where you tweak a resistor until the galvanometer reads exactly zero — that "null condition" lets you measure an unknown resistance precisely.

When Does Current Flow?

Closed loop: a complete path from battery + back to battery −. Current flows.
Open circuit: a break in the loop (e.g. open switch, broken wire). No current anywhere.
Short circuit: a wire bypasses the load and connects + directly to −. Huge current, extreme heating, wires glow or melt. In real life, this trips a fuse or breaker — or starts a fire.

Power — Where Does the Energy Go?

P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R
Power dissipated by a resistor or bulb, in watts. The "I² R" form shows why current squared matters: doubling current quadruples heat. This is the foundation of all electrical safety.

Theory — How Images Form

The Sign Convention

Every result below comes from one master formula. To use it on any lens or mirror, we need a consistent sign convention. This simulation uses the most common one in introductory physics:

u (object distance) is always positive — the object is in front of the element.
f (focal length) is positive for converging elements (convex lens, concave mirror), negative for diverging elements (concave lens, convex mirror).
v (image distance) is computed. Positive v means the image is real; negative v means the image is virtual.
m (magnification) = −v/u. Positive m = erect (right-side up); negative m = inverted (upside down).

The Master Equation

One equation governs all four curved elements. Plane mirrors are a trivial special case.

1/v = 1/f − 1/u ⟹ v = uf / (u − f)
Plug in u and the signed f. The sign of v tells you real vs virtual; the magnification m = −v/u tells you size and orientation.

Real vs Virtual — The Crucial Distinction

Real image: light rays actually converge at the image location. You can place a screen there and catch the image. Cameras, projectors, your retina, telescopes — all rely on real images.
Real images form on the opposite side of a lens (light passed through) or on the same side as the object for a mirror (light reflected back).
Virtual image: light rays only appear to come from the image location — they actually diverge after the element. You can't project a virtual image onto a screen, but you can see it by looking through/at the element. Magnifying glasses, eyeglasses, makeup mirrors, security mirrors — all show virtual images.
Virtual images form on the same side as the object for a lens, or behind a mirror.

The Five Cases of a Convex Lens (and Concave Mirror)

Converging elements have five different behaviours depending on where the object sits relative to the focal point. The concave mirror behaves identically, just reflected back rather than transmitted through.

Diverging Elements Are Simpler

A concave lens and a convex mirror have only one behaviour, no matter where you place the object:

Always Virtual · Always Erect · Always Diminished. The image is smaller than the object and on the same side (lens) or behind (mirror). This is why convex mirrors are used as security mirrors and car wing mirrors — they fit a wide field of view into a small reflection.

Plane Mirrors

The simplest case. A plane mirror has f = ∞, so the equation gives v = −u: the image is at the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front.

Always Virtual · Always Erect · Always Same Size. The only quirk is left-right reversal (handedness flip), which is a property of how mirrors fold space — not something captured in this 2D side-view.

How to Read a Ray Diagram

Three special rays are used to locate any image. Each follows a simple rule — you only need any two of them to find where the image lies, but drawing all three is a good check.

For a lens:
1. A ray parallel to the axis refracts through the far focal point.
2. A ray through the centre of the lens passes undeviated.
3. A ray through the near focal point emerges parallel to the axis.
For a mirror:
1. A ray parallel to the axis reflects through (or from) the focal point.
2. A ray through (or aimed at) the focal point reflects parallel to the axis.
3. A ray through (or aimed at) the centre of curvature (2F) reflects back on itself.

Where these reflected/refracted rays meet is where the image forms. If the rays diverge after the element, the image is virtual, and you find it by extending the rays backward until they meet — exactly what the dashed lines in the simulation show you.

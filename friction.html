Theory — Forces, Friction & Motion

Newton's Second Law — The Master Equation

Everything in this lab boils down to one statement: the net force on a body equals its mass times its acceleration. Applied to each body separately, with all forces accounted for, this single law solves every mechanics problem you'll meet in school.

ΣF = m × a
ΣF is the vector sum of all forces in newtons, m is mass in kg, a is acceleration in m/s². Sum forces along each axis separately. If a body is at rest or moving steadily, ΣF = 0.

Friction — The Self-Adjusting Force

Friction is the force that resists relative slipping between two surfaces in contact. It has two regimes:

Static friction acts when the surfaces are not slipping. It adjusts itself to exactly cancel whatever force is trying to start the slip — up to a maximum value: fs ≤ μs N. Push softly, friction pushes softly back. Push harder, friction matches you. Push hard enough that you exceed μs N — and the surface finally lets go.
Kinetic friction acts the moment the surfaces are slipping past each other. It is constant: fk = μk N. Once a block is sliding, friction stops adjusting and just opposes the motion with this fixed force.

For nearly every pair of surfaces, μs > μk. That's why a heavy box is hard to start moving — but once you've got it sliding, it suddenly becomes easier to keep going. Microscopically, atoms bond across the surface when at rest; sliding shears these bonds apart, leaving fewer to overcome.

Friction Does Not Depend on Contact Area

This trips up almost everyone. Two blocks of the same mass — one tall and thin, one wide and flat — sliding on the same surface experience the same friction. The formula has no area term:

f = μ × N
Smaller area means higher pressure at each point — but fewer points in contact. The two effects cancel. (At very low or very high pressures, or with soft materials like rubber tyres, the simple law breaks down — but for rigid surfaces in everyday conditions, it holds remarkably well.)

Angle of Repose — Measuring μs Without Instruments

Place a block on a slope and slowly tilt the slope. At a specific critical angle, the block starts to slip. Set up Newton's second law along and perpendicular to the slope, and you get a beautiful result:

tan(θc) = μs
Just measure the angle at which slipping begins, take its tangent — that is the coefficient of static friction. This is how physicists actually measure μs in the lab.

Why We Walk — Friction Is the Cause of Motion

Here is friction's deepest secret: it does not always oppose motion. It opposes relative slipping. On a stationary foot pushing the ground backward, the ground's reaction (by Newton's third law) pushes the foot forward. That forward push is friction — and it is what propels you.

On ice, there isn't enough friction. Your foot slides backward where you wanted to push, and you go nowhere. The same physics lets a car's driven wheel propel the car forward — the wheel pushes backward against the road, and friction (static, because the wheel does not actually slip on the road in normal driving) pushes the car forward.

Stacked Blocks & Internal Friction

When you pull the lower block, the upper one tends to be left behind by inertia. Friction at the interface between the blocks drags the upper one along. As long as the required friction force is below μs N, the two blocks move together as a single unit. If you accelerate too hard, the static limit is exceeded — and the upper block slips backward relative to the lower.

The Atwood Machine and Pulley Problems

Two masses connected by an inextensible string over a frictionless pulley feel the same magnitude of acceleration (the string constraint). Set up ΣF = ma for each mass separately, then solve simultaneously.

Standard Atwood (two hanging): a = (m₂ − m₁)g / (m₁ + m₂), T = 2 m₁ m₂ g / (m₁ + m₂)
If m₂ > m₁, m₂ falls and m₁ rises. The tension is the same throughout the string (massless ideal string and pulley).
Block on table + hanging mass with friction: a = (m₂ g − μ m₁ g) / (m₁ + m₂)
Only if the driving force m₂ g exceeds the friction limit μ m₁ g — otherwise the system stays at rest. Friction always points opposite to the direction of motion (or attempted motion).

Inclined Plane with Pulley

Block of mass m₁ on a slope of angle θ, connected over a pulley at the top to a hanging mass m₂. Compare the two driving tendencies — gravity component down the slope (m₁ g sin θ) versus the hanging weight (m₂ g) — adjusted by friction (μ m₁ g cos θ, opposing whichever way the system actually moves).

If m₂ g > m₁ g sinθ + μ m₁ g cosθ: a = (m₂ g − m₁ g sinθ − μ m₁ g cosθ) / (m₁ + m₂)
Hanging mass falls; block slides up the slope. Friction acts down the slope (opposing the motion of m₁).

Apparent Weight & Lifts

When you stand in an accelerating lift, the floor pushes up on you with a force N (the normal force) and your weight w = mg pulls down. Newton's second law gives N − mg = m a, where a is the lift's acceleration (positive upward).

Lift accelerating up → N = m(g + a), you feel heavier.
Lift moving at constant speed → N = mg, normal feeling.
Lift accelerating down → N = m(g − a), you feel lighter.
Lift in free fall (a = g) → N = 0, you feel weightless.

Free Fall & the Weightless Astronaut

An object in free fall — like a satellite in orbit — feels no normal force, no apparent weight. But gravity is still acting on it! That is why astronauts on the ISS float: not because there is no gravity (Earth's gravity at 400 km altitude is still ~89% of its surface value), but because both the astronaut and the spacecraft are in continuous free fall toward Earth — and missing — which is exactly what an orbit is.

Orbital condition: m v² / r = G M m / r² ⟹ v = √(G M / r)
For a satellite of mass m orbiting a body of mass M at radius r, the centripetal force requirement is supplied exactly by gravity. The satellite mass m cancels — every object at the same altitude needs the same orbital speed.
Low Earth orbit (r ≈ R_E): v ≈ √(g R_E) ≈ 7.9 km/s · T ≈ 90 min
Geostationary orbit (T = 24 h): r ≈ 42,200 km, altitude ≈ 35,800 km, v ≈ 3.07 km/s.

Problem-Solving Recipe

Every problem in this lab follows the same recipe:

1. Isolate each body. Draw its free body diagram.
2. Pick a convenient coordinate system (along the slope and perpendicular, for inclines).
3. Write ΣF = m a along each axis. Static cases give ΣF = 0.
4. For pulleys, the magnitudes of acceleration are equal (string is inextensible).
5. For friction, decide first whether it is static or kinetic. If static, the value is whatever it needs to be (up to μs N). If kinetic, it is exactly μk N.
6. Solve simultaneously.
